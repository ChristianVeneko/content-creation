# Sources: cloud_weight ("This Cloud Weighs 100 Elephants!")

Every fact in the voice, the captions or the picture, with where it is used and the page it came from.

| Fact | Used where | Source (read 2026-11-04) |
|---|---|---|
| A typical cumulus cloud holds about 500,000 kg (1.1 million pounds) of water | "500 tonnes", the 500 t drop | USGS Water Science School, "The Atmosphere and the Water Cycle" (usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/atmosphere-and-water-cycle) |
| Cumulus water density is about 0.5 g per cubic meter | "just half a gram of water" | same USGS page |
| A 1 km³ cloud contains 1 billion cubic meters | "holds a billion cubic meters", the counter | same USGS page |
| A typical cumulus is about a kilometer across and roughly cubical | "about a kilometer wide, tall and deep", the cube | Mental Floss, "How Much Does a Cloud Weigh?" (Peggy LeMone, NCAR) (mentalfloss.com/article/49786/how-much-does-cloud-weigh) |
| That cloud weighs about as much as 100 elephants | line 1, the title, the last line | same Mental Floss article; NPR (Robert Krulwich, with cloud scientist Andy Heymsfield) gives the same 100 elephants for a small puffy cloud (npr.org/transcripts/130078553) |
| Cloud droplets have radii of a few microns to a few tens of microns and are too small to feel gravity much | "droplets so tiny" | Scientific American, "Why do clouds float when they have tons of water in them?" (scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-clouds-float-when/) |
| A droplet of about 10 microns radius falls at about 1 cm per second (18-36 m per hour for 5-10 microns) | "they sink about a centimeter a second", the ruler and the pill | University of Wisconsin-Madison SSEC weather guys, "How fast do raindrops fall?" (wxguys.ssec.wisc.edu/2018/07/16/raindrop-speed); Live Science, "Why do clouds float?" |
| Rising air in convective clouds exceeds a few meters per second and offsets the droplets' slow fall | "warm air rising ... pushes up hundreds of times faster", "a few m per second" | Scientific American (above) |
| The air in a small cumulus cloud's volume weighs about 1,000 times more than its water | "weighs about 1,000 times more than the water", the balance | Scientific American (above) |
| A raindrop (about a millimeter across) is about a million times heavier than the cloud droplets that make it | "A million of them make one raindrop" | NASA Earth Observatory media alert, 2006 (webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080923042151/http://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/Newsroom/MediaAlerts/2006/2006120823849.html); USGS: "millions of cloud droplets are required to produce a single raindrop" |

## Left out
- The exact elephant weight behind "100 elephants": sources use 4 to 6 tonnes, so the video never says one.
- Storm clouds and hurricanes in elephants (200,000 and 15-40 million): the numbers vary a lot between sources.
- "Moist air is less dense than dry air" (USGS's own explanation of floating): true, but a smaller effect than the updrafts and droplet size, so it would muddy the story.
- Exact updraft speeds: they range from a few cm/s in flat clouds to meters per second in puffy ones, so the video only says "a few meters per second".
